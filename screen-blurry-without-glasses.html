Guides / Fixing a blurry screen

Screen blurry without your glasses? Here's what actually helps.

Updated July 2026 · 7 minute read · By the DOA team

Search this problem and you'll find the same recycled list: turn up brightness, adjust the sharpness slider, enable night mode. None of those touch the actual cause. The blur isn't in your monitor. It's in the optics between the screen and your retina, and only things that address those optics will genuinely change what you see.

First, know which blur you have

The fix depends on the cause, and there are four common ones:

  • Near-sightedness (myopia): things beyond a certain distance blur. Your screen is blurry because it sits beyond your far point, the edge of your sharp zone. Small text goes soft and doubling appears at the edges of letters.
  • Far-sightedness (hyperopia): close-up focus takes effort. The screen may look readable at first, then swim and tire your eyes as the session goes on.
  • Astigmatism: blur with a direction. Letters smear or ghost along one axis, and bright elements streak. We wrote a separate guide on astigmatism and screens.
  • Presbyopia (reading glasses age): close focus stiffens with age, so screens at reading distance get harder every year, even if your distance vision is perfect.

If the blur is new, sudden, or got noticeably worse recently, stop reading and book an eye test. Fresh blur is a question for a professional, not for settings.

The usual advice, graded honestly

Turn up brightness Doesn't fix blur

Brightness changes how much light reaches you, not how it focuses. A brighter blurry screen is just a brighter blur. There's one indirect benefit: bright light shrinks your pupils, and a smaller pupil slightly increases depth of focus (the same reason squinting helps). The effect is real but small, and cranking brightness invites its own eye strain.

The monitor's "sharpness" setting Wrong kind of sharp

Monitor sharpness adds edge contrast to the signal. It cannot pre-compensate for your eyes; it just exaggerates edges, which often looks worse through optical blur, not better. The blur happens after the light leaves the screen. A setting that changes the image before that point, without knowing your prescription, is guessing.

Night mode / blue light filters Different problem

Warm tinting may feel gentler in the evening, but it has nothing to do with focus. Text is exactly as blurry in orange as in blue.

Bigger text and display scaling Genuinely useful

Scaling doesn't remove blur, but it makes text survive blur. A letter twice as tall loses a smaller fraction of its shape to the same optical smear. On Windows: Ctrl and + in browsers, or Settings → Display → Scale. If you do only one free thing from this page, do this.

Move the screen Works if you're near-sighted

If you're myopic, everything inside your far point is sharp. Divide 1 metre by your prescription: −2.00 means sharp within 50cm, −3.00 within 33cm. Bring the screen inside that range and the blur is simply gone, no software required. The limit is posture and comfort, and it stops being practical somewhere around −3.50 and stronger.

Correct the display to your prescription Addresses the cause

The remaining option is newer: keep the screen where it is, and adjust what it displays using your actual glasses prescription, so the image lands sharper for your specific eyes. That's what DOA does on Windows. You enter the values from your prescription (SPH, CYL, AXIS, ADD), take your glasses off, and DOA adjusts the display for the distance you set. It runs on the GPU across every app, and there's a free tier to test it on your own screen.

Honesty section, because this matters: it only fixes blur you actually have. If your screen is already sharp barefaced at your distance, there's nothing to correct and you'll barely notice it working. The effect is subtle by design, and for near-sighted users the sweet spot is roughly −2.00 to −4.50 at a typical desk distance; beyond that, screen resolution becomes the limiting factor. Sitting a little closer shifts that window deeper, which can bring stronger prescriptions back into range. It's also a comfort tool for screen use, not a replacement for your glasses in the rest of your life.

The 10-second test

Glasses off, normal sitting position. If this sentence just went soft, that's optical blur, and it's the kind of thing prescription-based correction is built for. If it's still crisp, your screen distance is inside your sharp zone and you mostly don't have this problem.

The order to try things

  • Blur is new or changing? Eye test first. Everything else assumes a stable, known prescription.
  • Free wins first: scale your text up, and if you're mildly myopic, pull the screen closer.
  • Still straining? Try prescription-based display correction and judge it with the toggle test: switch it off and on while reading small text. Comparison shows the difference far better than staring at a corrected screen in isolation.
